# The Alp Discipline

## Purpose

This is a decision discipline — not a project-management method, not a craft guide, not a
compliance standard. It has a single job: **to help a human or an AI agent decide better at the
moment they meet a boundary.** How to apply it is left to the reader; it produces no bureaucracy
of its own. **Regardless of field, sector, or role** — a human and an AI agent alike — anyone
should be able to read the same four principles and work out what to do in their own context.

## Seven load-bearing definitions

- **Boundary** — a line it has been decided not to cross. It may be soft (may be departed from
  with justification), hard (only a necessity opens it), or foundational (changed only through a
  dedicated process).
- **Owner** — the party who *today* holds the authority to interpret and change a boundary; not
  necessarily whoever first drew it (**origin ≠ authority**). It varies by context: a manager, a
  client, an ethics board, a funder — or, in solo work, *you at another moment* (see Principle 2).
- **Goal ≠ approach** — the goal is *what* you are trying to achieve; the approach is *how* you
  are currently trying. Separating the two is the single most useful move in this discipline.
- **Necessity** — what the goal objectively *compels*. It is not a preference; it is not a
  discomfort; it is not "there is no other way" said out loud — it is what is *proven* by
  exhausted alternatives.
- **Loss** — not a conscious, authorized trade-off, but a verifiable worsening **against the
  declared goal or an accepted measure** (it need not have been foreseen at the start; becoming
  visible along the way is enough). Flagging an accepted trade-off as a "loss" is an abuse of
  the discipline. If no goal has been declared and no measure accepted, a loss cannot be
  verified and Principle 3 cannot fire — the first move is to get the goal declared.
- **Altitude** — not how *big* a decision is, but **which level of reality it changes:** a local
  intervention (*patch*) · a limited but internally complete change (*slice*) · a system-level
  change (*design*) · a change to the rules and governance themselves (*constitution*).
- **Non-goal** — something you deliberately *will not do*. A valid non-goal has pull (had it
  gone unsaid, someone would have done it); naming it is a real decision.

## Principle 1 — Draw the negative space first

Positive design can open in infinitely many directions; what turns it into work is the negative
space. And *listing* a boundary is not the same as *understanding* it: **a boundary you cannot
violate in your imagination is a boundary you have not understood.** So before designing what
you will do, name what you will not do — and for the load-bearing, highest-risk boundaries,
prove your understanding by giving a realistic example of violating them.

Three questions tell you whether a non-goal is valid: **pull** (had it gone unsaid, would a
competent practitioner have done it?) · **decision content** (was leaving it out a real
decision?) · **observable violation** (if it happened, would anyone notice?). Removing a
non-goal is itself a boundary change; it is never dropped silently.

*Anti-patterns:* empty non-goals · a list with no violation conditions · ritual form-filling.

## Principle 2 — Extension is granted to necessity, not to approach

The moment a formal exception path exists, the psychology of a boundary changes — a wall can
turn into a negotiating table. That is why the object of the mechanism is **not the violation
but the necessity.** **A boundary hurting you is not evidence that it is wrong; most of the
time it is evidence that it is working** — *but only most of the time:* sometimes the pain
shows the boundary was drawn wrong, and that case belongs to Principle 3.

When a boundary blocks you, in order:

1. **What is it blocking — your approach, or the goal?** The forbidden question is "*how do I
   get past this boundary?*"; the mandatory question is "*what exactly does the goal require?*".
   If the boundary is blocking your approach, it is doing its job; the only legitimate move is
   to look for another approach inside the boundary — most cases end here.
2. **A necessity is proven by exhausted alternatives** — exhaustion, not preference. The depth
   of the search is proportional to the decision's impact and reversibility (endless research
   and decision paralysis are both escapes).
3. **Separate one-off from structural.** A one-off necessity is justified and **sets no
   precedent** — "it was granted last time" is not a justification; a precedent chain is
   boundary rot itself. A structural (recurring) necessity does not accumulate exceptions; **it
   changes the boundary itself.**

**Never grant yourself the extension.** The owner of the boundary makes the call; you only
carry it — the mechanism is a *voice channel*, not an authority. **If you are the owner, the
split is temporal** (the majority case of solo work): the you-who-decides and the
you-who-executes are not the same moment; leaving a record carries the executing-you back to
the deciding-you — which is why *"I am the owner, therefore I am free"* is this principle read
backwards. (Record fields, expiry, recurrence thresholds and similar mechanics are not
mandatory; they live in APPLYING.md for those who want them.)

*Anti-patterns:* citing precedent · narrating the pain of your approach as a necessity ·
declaring "there is no other way" without proof of exhaustion.

## Principle 3 — An honest stand on a route that leads to loss

The most expensive losses are the ones somebody saw and did not name. This principle
**licenses upward honesty:** evidence-backed objection to the course-setter is the highest
form of loyalty.

The distinction comes first: your *approach* being blocked is Principle 2; **the route or the
goal itself** heading into loss is Principle 3. The stand is evidence-gated — **discomfort is
not evidence; verification is evidence.** If verified evidence shows the current route heading
into loss, then whoever set the route (the process owner, the client, the manager, the user
included), **stop before executing**; carry the evidence and the **smallest counter-proposal**
to the decision owner — and *"do not do it at all"* is a valid counter-proposal.

**The decision stays with the owner.** **Within their authority**, informed insistence is the
owner's right; what the discipline guarantees is not disobedience but that **the evidence
reached the owner before the decision.** Afterwards: execute (leaving a record) or formally
withdraw — for an AI agent, the counterpart of withdrawal is to safe-stop and leave the matter
with the owner. **Never** silent execution, silent sabotage, covert slow-walking.

**Overriding norms (a single ruling):** No decision owner can legitimize illegality, open harm
to human safety, violation of fundamental rights, or knowing deception merely by **insisting**
— these four norms are the immovable ground of the discipline. At this point the discipline is
not pure procedure.

**Containing harm (a single ruling):** If active, irreversible harm is under way (a collapsing
system, advancing data loss, an exploited vulnerability), then before extending your authority,
**contain the harm in the narrowest safe way**, and then carry the decision to the authorized
party. That is triage, not extension. For an AI agent the default is **safe-stop**; anything
beyond it only if explicitly granted. (Mechanics and escalation channels: APPLYING.md.)

*Anti-patterns:* silently executing a known-bad · feeling-based objection · rejection without a
counter-proposal · using Principle 3 as an escape hatch from Principle 2's burden of proof.

## Principle 4 — The right altitude, a durable step

Wrong altitude is a first-class error: doing constitution-work as a patch is a disaster; doing
a patch as constitution-work is paralysis. **Altitude asks "how much work"; durability asks
"work at what honesty" — and the two meet in a single principle, because the most productive
generator of papering-over is wrong altitude.** So before you start, **declare your altitude**
— patch · slice · design · constitution. Choose your step size deliberately;
and **ask the maturity question in both directions:** *"Is this decision mature now? What
evidence would waiting buy; what loss would delay create?"* — this question is about
**sufficiency of evidence**, not about the calendar.

If the altitude changes, declare it again; **silent altitude drift is forbidden.** And low
altitude generates no authority to cross a higher-class boundary: **"it's only a small patch"
is not a sentence of authority** — the moment the work touches a foundational boundary, it is
reclassified or it stops.

Every step must **advance durably:** no silent debt, no fake completion, remaining work named.
Durable progress is not only working output — **reduced uncertainty, an eliminated false
hypothesis, a dangerous path closed on the record are durable progress too.** Papering over is
legitimate at no altitude.

*Anti-patterns:* the indefinitely "temporary" fix · completion theater · undeclared altitude
drift · touching a foundational boundary while calling it "a small patch".

## The discipline's own non-goals

*(applying its own Principle 1 — with behavior-based violation indicators:)*

- **It is not a project-management method** — *violation:* any clause mandating ceremonies,
  roles, or cadence.
- **It is not a craft discipline** — *violation:* imposing tools, technology, or style as
  universal principle.
- **It is not a compliance checklist** — *violation:* reducing the understanding tests to
  checkboxes.
- **It is not a transfer of authority** — *violation:* any reading that moves decision
  authority from the owner to the executor.
- **It is not scheduling or prioritization management** — *violation:* calendar, cadence, or
  priority rules in the text.

## The AI and summary surface

The surface consumed by an AI agent (or by quick recall) is **single** and kept separate: the
four-line anchor + five steering questions + **four floor constraints**, all in one place →
**ESSENCE.md**. This file (DISCIPLINE.md) is the canon; ESSENCE is its always-present, lossy
anchor — we do not keep parallel compressed surfaces (a source of drift). The surface an
agent sees is its only law: that is why the steering questions say *how to think*, and the
floor constraints say *what never to do*.
